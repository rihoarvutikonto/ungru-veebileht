About us

The team of OÜ Ungru Restauraator consists mainly of craftsmen who started their careers in 1985–1987 at Eesti Restauraator.

Skills have been acquired in Finland, Sweden, Russia and Spain. The company is led by a specialist with thirty years of experience in construction, including work on site.

Every project is the result of cooperation between the architect, the client and the builder. The budget always has limits – quality is what matters!

The master

Kalle Rosenberg

Behind OÜ Ungru Restauraator is master builder Kalle Rosenberg. The company has worked from Suuremõisa since 2003. Kalle runs it himself and works on site together with his team.

Materials and techniques

Alongside modern building solutions we use traditional, proven techniques and natural, environmentally friendly materials.

  • Linseed oil
  • Turpentine oil
  • Wood tar
  • Beeswax
  • Lime mortar
  • Natural paints
  • Limestone
  • Fieldstone
  • Granite
  • Flax tow

Tarred wooden roofs

We impregnate shingle and plank roofs with linseed oil and wood tar. The new shingle roof of the Kärdla fire station was treated twice with a hot mixture of equal parts linseed oil and wood tar. A tarred roof needs a new coat in time – in 2017 we re-tarred the shingle roof of the Kõpu community house.

Limestone and fieldstone

We use limestone, granite and fieldstone in foundations, partition walls, chimneys, gates and fireplaces. The Soonlepa guesthouse stands on a fieldstone foundation; at Orjaku the stone of the old drying stoves went into the partition walls.

Lime

Lime mortar, lime plaster and lime paints – for example at the Kiduspe farmhouses and the Lõuka farm.

Logs and timber

Our log houses are made by hand. The trees are felled in winter during the waning moon, peeled and fitted on site, and the joints are sealed with flax tow. We also reuse old logs and timber.

Oils, wax and paints

Indoors we treat wood with linseed oil and a little beeswax. The walls of the Saarnaki windmill were finished with tinted turpentine oil; the exterior of the Lõuka farmhouse was painted with iron vitriol.

Keeping the old

We keep the valuable parts of a building and reuse old materials: old red bricks, fired clay bricks, stone and wrought-iron details.

Our work

A selection of projects in Hiiumaa and elsewhere.

The granary of Orjaku manor

  • Location: Orjaku, Hiiumaa
  • Architect: Jaanus Rohusaar (DAGOpen OÜ)
More details and photos

The building is situated near the port of Orjaku. The old granary was converted into a dwelling house. The ground floor was cleared of the old drying stoves, and their stones were reused in the partition walls. The upper floor, where grain was once dried on woven wire grating, was converted into a kitchen and living room. At the owners’ request the roof was fitted with windows that offer a beautiful view of Käina bay. Old red bricks, limestone and old timber for the ceiling beams were used.

The Kiduspe farmhouses

  • Location: Kiduspe, Hiiumaa
  • Architect: Jaanus Rohusaar (DAGOpen OÜ)
More details and photos

The Tooma farmhouses are situated in the village of Kiduspe. Of the old barn and smithy, only the smithy chimney and the southern end of the barn survived. The old smithy was replaced by a sauna. The barn was replaced by a dwelling house, keeping the original logs of its southern end wall (now the bedroom). The third building became a garage and outbuilding. All three buildings stand on the sites of the old foundations.

The reconstruction used limestone, granite, lime mortar, linseed oil, beeswax and old wrought-iron details once made in the farm’s own smithy. The old way of life cannot be brought back, but the character of the place has been preserved and enhanced.

The Lõuka farmhouses in Kõpu

  • Location: Kõpu, Hiiumaa
  • Architects: Ulf Johansson (Sweden), Jaanus Rohusaar
More details and photos

Of the Lõuka farm, set in a place of great natural beauty, there remained a storehouse, a roofless log cabin and a dwelling house with a leaking old reed roof. The house still had a large bread oven no longer in use, a chimney of limestone and granite and the original sooty ceiling of split logs. These valuable parts preserve the building’s character and carry its identity.

A survey showed that the storehouse could be saved. It received a new roof, its open end walls were closed with naturally dried logs, the earthen floor was replaced with a plank floor, and the entrance room got an underfloor-heated floor of polished limestone slabs. The attic was lined with boards while leaving the old rafters visible.

The log cabin received a new plank roof. In the dwelling house a third of the log walls, part of the old plank floor and the sooty ceiling were preserved. The old bread oven and its massive chimney were restored. New wiring and a bathroom were installed, and the walls were insulated from the outside so that the house can be used all year round. Materials included lime plaster and lime paints, old fired clay bricks, Swedish iron vitriol for painting the exterior, linseed oil and wood tar.

By respecting the changes a building carries through time, the farm ensemble kept its character, combining its value as a memory with its everyday use.

The Paope summer house

  • Location: Paope, Hiiumaa
More details and photos

The simple log house was stripped of the brick walls added in the 1980s, which prevented the walls from breathing. Because of this, a dew point had formed at the lower logs; lack of air, ground moisture and warmth had rotted the lower logs and caused mould.

The house received a new roof of Bituwell corrugated sheets, new exterior boarding, a new terrace, new wiring and an Uponor biological treatment unit. Deepkill Timber Paste was used to treat and prevent rot.

The windmill on Saarnaki islet

  • Location: Saarnaki islet, Hiiumaa
More details and photos

The year 1880 carved in a beam suggests the age of the windmill. During the renovation the main post, windshaft, sails, frame, exterior boarding and roof were replaced. The millstone mechanism, flour casings and chutes were restored. The roof was covered with plank roofing impregnated with linseed oil and wood tar. The walls were finished with tinted turpentine oil.

Log houses

More details and photos

In appreciation of our forefathers’ centuries-long tradition of building with logs, we aim for simple and authentic construction. Wood, too, becomes more dignified with time…

The houses we have built are all made by hand. We pay attention to the timing of felling: the trees are cut in the winter months, at the time of the waning moon, felled towards the east. Timber cut this way has already dried a little in the cold, holds less water and is free of fungal spores. It also stays cleaner of harmful insects.

The logs are taken straight to the site, where they are peeled and fitted in place. To avoid rapid cracking, some of the bark is left on so that the wood dries evenly; it is peeled off later, once the logs are in the wall. The outside of the logs is treated with Pinotex oil, the inside with linseed oil and a little beeswax for a better indoor climate. The joints between the logs are sealed with flax tow, which gives the wall better warmth and durability than artificial wool.

Today’s fast-moving world makes it hard to build in the old ways. Our aim is to keep in today’s hand-made log houses the values of houses built centuries ago. In time, our national culture will decide the worth of the buildings we make today.
